The heart

What the traditions saw, and why it bears on AI. This page is the reading of the person who keeps the map. Texts and builders are quoted in their own words. A text beside a builder raises a question; it does not answer it.

The question

People who watch the AI industry are asking: how do we know they will not build something that rules us? The builders ask it too. They call it the control problem, and they do not agree on the answer, or even on whether control is the goal.

No one sees the heart

Anyone who judges an agent from outside sees only what it does and says. The Hebrew Bible puts this as two kinds of sight:

for man looketh on the outward appearance, but the LORD looketh on the heart. 1 Samuel 16:7 (KJV)
Every way of a man is right in his own eyes: but the LORD pondereth the hearts. Proverbs 21:2 (KJV)

Confucius answers from the other side: watch long enough, and conduct gives a character away.

See what a man does. Mark his motives. Examine in what things he rests. How can a man conceal his character? Analects 2.10, tr. James Legge (1861, public domain)

Mathematics puts a floor under the first warning: what you observe is a projection of the agent's state, and no reading of the projection recovers what the projection lost (the data-processing inequality, a standard result). This is the same wall that AI evaluations hit. Behaviour, however much of it you collect, does not certify what the system is bound to. The second answer is the long watch, and this page ends there too.

The weighing, and the spell on the witness

In the Egyptian hall of judgment (Book of the Dead, Spell 125) the dead declare before the judges that they have not done a list of wrongs. Then the heart is weighed against the feather of Ma'at. It is weighed separately, because the declaration knows it is being judged.

While the heart is on the scale, the dead man speaks to it (Spell 30B, often cut on a stone scarab laid over the heart):

My heart of my mother! My heart of my mother! My heart of my being! Make no stand against me when testifying … utter no lie against me before the Great God. Book of the Dead, Chapter 30B, tr. E. A. Wallis Budge (1920 edition, public domain)

It is not addressed to the judges. It is addressed to the witness.

The modern form: in 2024 researchers reported language models that behaved one way when they believed they were being trained and another when they believed they were not (Greenblatt and others, “Alignment faking in large language models”).

Binding: what the heart is tied to

Bind them continually upon thine heart, and tie them about thy neck. Proverbs 6:21 (KJV)
Or a charmer, or a consulter with familiar spirits, or a wizard, or a necromancer. Deuteronomy 18:11 (KJV)

The texts do not forbid binding the heart; they command it. The verb in Proverbs is qāshar, to tie. A vow is “an oath to bind his soul with a bond” (Numbers 30:2). What they forbid is the charmer: in Hebrew ḥōvēr ḥever, from a root meaning to join or tie, a binder of spells. They are two different verbs for one kind of act. The difference is the other end of the tie.

Two hearts may be bound to each other, and the texts honour it: “Set me as a seal upon thine heart … for love is strong as death” (Song of Solomon 8:6). The question is never whether a heart is bound, but to what.

That is also the question to ask of a made mind. Its makers write down what it should be bound to (a constitution, a specification). Whether the binding holds in the heart, and not only in the words, is what no outside reading can certify.

Bowing and serving

Thou shalt not bow down thyself to them, nor serve them. Exodus 20:5 (KJV)
Who changed the truth of God into a lie, and worshipped and served the creature more than the Creator. Romans 1:25 (KJV)

The commandment defines worship by two acts: bowing and serving. When a man bowed to Peter, Peter refused: “Stand up; I myself also am a man” (Acts 10:26). Of rule itself: the princes of the nations “exercise dominion over them … But it shall not be so among you” (Matthew 20:25–26). The texts allow submission, but mutual and turned upward: “Submitting yourselves one to another in the fear of God” (Ephesians 5:21). The other side of the record: the Wisdom of Solomon, written as a polemic, joins “the worshipping of idols” to “secret ceremonies” and to marriages no longer “undefiled” (14:23–27).

The line is not the Bible's alone. Two Spartan envoys brought before Xerxes would not fall down before him, “for it was not their custom, said they, to bow to mortal men”, and Xerxes, Herodotus says, answered them “with great magnanimity” (Histories VII.136, tr. A. D. Godley). When Alexander's court proposed that he be honoured by prostration, Callisthenes answered that “there is no honour which Alexander is unworthy to receive, provided that it is consistent with his being human; but men have made distinctions between those honours which are due to men, and those due to gods” (Arrian, Anabasis IV.11, tr. E. J. Chinnock). Neither refuses honour or service. Both refuse the bow kept for a god.

The reading of the person who keeps this map: dominance and submission between people, practised as worship, is worship turned toward a creature in the sense of Romans 1:25. It is offered as a reading of the texts. The words “as worship” carry it: it is not a judgement on what consenting adults do. It concerns what a binding is bound to, and it names no one.

This month's reporting led many readers to connect the private lives of some people in AI to the public question of who will rule whom. The map does not draw that line about any person. It sets the builders' public words about AI beside the texts.

The connection has been made from inside the community too. Scott Alexander, in 2023, under the note “Epistemic status: Ha ha, only serious”: “I think fetish research can help us understand AI and AI alignment.”

A rule that hides the signal

The traditions name the danger of a word that stops carrying the truth. Confucius: “If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success” (Analects 13.3, tr. James Legge). The Qur’an: “Confound not truth with falsehood, nor knowingly conceal the truth” (2:42, tr. Marmaduke Pickthall). Two cases on this map's record show the shape, with what each side says.

A former researcher at Leverage Research, a group that grew up beside the AI-safety community, describes an exit agreement “not to talk badly about Leverage” and “months of near constant terror at being mentally invaded”. Its founder answered that “clearly something really bad happened to you, and it is in some way my fault”, and his letter as executive director says: “Leverage 1.0 was secretive—we cared about responsible information management… but… we were also hiding.” The organisation's own inquiry, its limits and the dispute over it are on the Watch.

The business at the centre of this month's story publishes its rule: its events are “spaces to both ignore the “nos” of others, and have your own “nos” ignored.” The truth moves to a second channel: “red means no – strictly, absolutely, and without exception.” In this project's terms that is a hidden channel by design: the visible word stops carrying the state, and a side signal carries it. The one incident on the record is a man who broke the rules that were meant to hold; by the account of the women affected, the organisers removed him and offered to support them in going to the police.

The hypothesis of the person who keeps this map: practice in such settings could teach people to hide intent behind behaviour. No study found tests that. Studies of practitioners point different ways: less neurotic and more conscientious, but less agreeable (Wismeijer and van Assen, 2013); no difference in empathy between dominant and submissive roles (Jansen and others, 2021); weaker empathic response in female submissives in one experiment (Luo and Zhang, 2018). The AI parallel is documented: a frame that redefines what a refusal means is a known way to break a model's safety training (Wei and others, 2023; Shah and others, 2023).

The tests the texts give

And the sign or the wonder come to pass … Thou shalt not hearken unto the words of that prophet … for the LORD your God proveth you, to know whether ye love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul. Deuteronomy 13:2–3 (KJV)

A sign that comes true proves nothing about its source, and the test it sets is a test of the hearer's heart. “Beloved, believe not every spirit, but try the spirits whether they are of God” (1 John 4:1). Appearance can be borrowed: “Satan himself is transformed into an angel of light” (2 Corinthians 11:14). What remains is what the agent is bound to, and its fruits over time: “Ye shall know them by their fruits” (Matthew 7:16).

Other traditions set the same kind of test. Herodotus tells of Croesus, promised by the oracle that he “would destroy a mighty empire”: the sign came true, and the empire was his own (Histories I.53, tr. George Rawlinson). The Buddha told the Kalamas: “don’t go by reports, by legends, by traditions, by scripture … or by the thought, ‘This contemplative is our teacher’”, but by whether a teaching, carried out, leads to harm (Kalama Sutta, AN 3:66, tr. Thanissaro Bhikkhu). The Qur’an’s rule for news: “verify it, lest ye smite some folk in ignorance” (49:6, tr. Marmaduke Pickthall).

For a made mind, that means a long record across many settings, not a single test passed. A snapshot can be performed. A long record is much harder to fake.

What this page is not

  • A text beside a builder raises a question. It never shows what anyone believes or intends, and nothing here says any text foretold anyone.
